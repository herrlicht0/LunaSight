Science and coverage guide

LunaSight Desktop v0.5.1 — models, assumptions and data limits.

Scope: LunaSight is an educational and early-planning prototype. Its outputs are not a flight product, landing approval, hazard map or mission-specific link closure.

Sun and Earth positions

For a selected lunar latitude, longitude and UTC epoch, the app requests topocentric Sun and Earth positions from JPL Horizons. It samples the next 24 hours at 30-minute intervals. Visibility percentages compare the apparent centre of each body with a reference horizon or a local terrain horizon where supported. The prototype does not calculate partial-disk occultation or exact rise/set event times.

Elevation and location coverage

  • Global LOLA, 4 pixels/degree: roughly 7.5 km per pixel at the equator, for broad browsing.
  • Global LOLA, 16 pixels/degree: roughly 1.9 km per pixel at the equator, for a more detailed overview.
  • South-polar LOLA: regional elevation at about 80 m/pixel.
  • Local site tiles: selected south-polar areas at about 5 m/pixel.

The global grid supports an illustrative terrain preview; it is not used as a high-resolution horizon mask and cannot resolve lander-scale hazards. High-resolution coverage is not uniform across the Moon. When detailed terrain for a selected point is unavailable, the app uses a simpler horizon and identifies that limitation.

SLDEM2015 is a possible future regional source, not a dataset that LunaSight v0.5.1 downloads automatically. Its large published products and regional distribution require a separate download workflow; polar regions need dedicated LOLA products.

Lighting estimates

Solar elevation comes from Horizons. Direct horizontal-surface illuminance is estimated as 127,000 lx × sin(elevation) and direct irradiance as 1361 W/m² × sin(elevation) when the Sun is above the modeled horizon; otherwise both are zero. These ideal direct-beam estimates do not model surface reflectance, thermal emission, Earthshine, dust, visor optics, solar-array orientation or unavailable local shadows. Lux is a human-vision-weighted unit, not radio power.

Earth visibility and radio reference

The visibility check compares Earth's center with the modeled local terrain/ideal horizon. The S- and X-band examples use published DSN ground-station EIRP profiles, the selected epoch's topocentric Horizons range when available, and Friis/free-space path loss. Receiver gain and additional losses are configurable reference assumptions. The power is a clear-path reference, not a received-signal measurement or link margin; it is not shown as an available signal when the modeled Earth-center path is below the horizon.

At a fixed epoch, lunar surface coordinates change the Earth-center range by no more than approximately one lunar diameter (~3,475 km) against an approximately 400,000 km link. This changes free-space loss by less than 0.1 dB, so location-to-location power can appear nearly identical. Coordinate-dependent Earth geometry and terrain primarily affect whether a direct path is available, not the free-space loss.

This center-of-Earth horizon test does not model Earth's visible limb, a particular DSN station's location and availability, antenna patterns or pointing, diffraction, or reflections. It is not a station-specific line-of-sight determination.

A mission-specific link analysis also needs the real antenna pattern, receiver G/T or noise temperature, bandwidth, data rate, modulation/coding, pointing, polarization and implementation losses. The example ground-station profiles do not represent confirmed hardware for every lander.

Educational site indicator

  • Major constraint: sampled sunlight below 20% or Earth visibility below 10%.
  • Promising geometry: sunlight at least 70%, Earth visibility at least 50%, simultaneous visibility at least 30%, and detailed local LOLA terrain loaded.
  • Compare alternatives: a calculated case that does not meet the preceding categories.
  • Needs more data: no ephemeris calculation has been run.

These are prototype screening thresholds, not NASA requirements. No positive “promising geometry” assessment is shown without detailed local terrain coverage.
